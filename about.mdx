The *Encyclopedia of Rhetoric and Composition* is a one-volume reference work dedicated to the art, history, and theory of rhetoric — a discipline that is simultaneously the oldest of the humanities and one of the most actively contested fields in contemporary scholarship. This digital edition makes that scholarship freely navigable, searchable, and conversational, preserving the full editorial content of the original print publication while extending it with modern tools for discovery and research.

## Editorial History

The encyclopedia was edited by **Theresa Enos**, a leading figure in twentieth-century rhetoric and composition studies, and first published in 1996 as part of the *Garland Reference Library of the Humanities* (Vol. 1389) by Routledge. Enos brought together a wide range of contributors to produce what she described as an introduction to rhetoric that could also serve as a map of the field — its major periods, its key personages, its foundational concepts, and its contested applications.

In her editor's note, Enos acknowledged the inherent challenge of the project:

> *"Confining the spaciousness of rhetoric and its 2,500-year history of theory and praxis into one volume cannot completely capture rhetoric's metadisciplinary nature. This Encyclopedia can suggest the scope of rhetoric as it is studied in different disciplines in American higher education and give future direction in communicating within and across complex, multilayered discourse communities."*

That candor about limitation is itself a rhetorical act — and a fitting one for a reference work that asks readers to treat it as a beginning, not an end.

<Note>
  The digital edition preserves all original editorial content from the 1996 Routledge publication. No articles have been added, removed, or substantively edited. The AI assistant and knowledge graph are tools layered on top of this corpus, not replacements for it.
</Note>

## Scope and Coverage

The encyclopedia spans 2,500 years of rhetorical thought, from the sophists of ancient Athens to the emergence of digital discourse and network communication at the end of the twentieth century.

- **274 entries** organized across **23 letter sections** (A–Y, excluding J and X)
- Coverage extends from **ancient Greece and Rome** through the medieval, Renaissance, Enlightenment, and modern periods to the **digital age**
- Rhetoric is treated as a metadiscipline, drawing on anthropology, linguistics, philosophy, psychology, literary theory, and communication studies
- Entries address terms, figures, movements, genres, methodologies, and theoretical frameworks

## The Four Entry Types

Entries in the encyclopedia are not uniform in length or depth. They are classified into four types, each serving a distinct purpose for different kinds of readers and research needs.

<Accordion title="Identifications">
  Brief definitions of a rhetorical figure, term, or concept. Identifications are the encyclopedia's most compact entries — typically one to three paragraphs — designed to orient a reader quickly without requiring prior knowledge of the field.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Notes">
  Elaborated explanations that go beyond definition to provide context, trace the development of an idea, or situate a concept within broader rhetorical discourse. Notes are especially useful when a term carries contested or historically layered meanings.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Essays">
  In-depth explorations of major subjects in rhetoric and composition. Essays survey the scholarly literature, trace historical development, and address competing interpretations. They typically include a bibliography of primary and secondary sources.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Articles">
  The encyclopedia's most substantial entries, offering full scholarly treatments of rhetoric's most significant terms, movements, and methodologies. Articles are written for readers who need a thorough, research-level introduction to a subject, and they include extensive cross-references to related entries.
</Accordion>

## The Digital Edition

While the editorial content comes directly from the 1996 Routledge publication, the digital edition adds a set of tools designed to make that content more accessible and more useful for contemporary researchers, students, and curious readers.

- **Full-text search** — every word in every entry is indexed and searchable in real time (⌘K / Ctrl+K)
- **AI chat assistant** powered by Claude 3.5 Sonnet — ask questions about rhetoric in plain language and receive answers grounded in the encyclopedia's articles
- **Interactive knowledge graph** — a visual map of relationships between entries, allowing you to navigate the encyclopedia as a network of ideas rather than a linear list
- **Alphabetical browsing** — the full 274-entry corpus organized into 23 letter sections, accessible from the sidebar at any time
- **LLM-friendly API endpoints** — machine-readable access to encyclopedia content, designed for developers, researchers, and large language model integrations
