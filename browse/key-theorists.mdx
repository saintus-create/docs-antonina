Rhetoric has been defined, disputed, and reinvented by an extraordinary succession of thinkers across 2,500 years. The encyclopedia includes dedicated entries for more than thirty individual theorists, from the reputed founder of the discipline in fifth-century Sicily to twentieth-century scholars who brought rhetoric into dialogue with linguistics, psychoanalysis, feminism, and deconstruction. The entries below are organised roughly by period; each name links to a full article that covers the theorist's life, major works, and contribution to the discipline.

<Note>
  Most theorist entries include a **See Also** section listing related encyclopedia articles. If you arrive at a theorist entry and want to explore the broader intellectual context, those cross-references are the fastest route to connected topics.
</Note>

## Ancient Greek Rhetoricians

<CardGroup cols={2}>
  <Card title="Corax (c. 490–440 BCE)" icon="landmark">
    Credited as the founder of rhetoric for formalising systems of public expression in Syracuse, Sicily, around 467 BCE. His work with Tisias established the first systematic approach to persuasive argument in the law courts.
  </Card>

  <Card title="Isocrates" icon="school">
    Fourth-century Athenian rhetor and educator whose school rivalled Plato's Academy. His model of rhetorical education — blending ethics, civic purpose, and stylistic excellence — influenced liberal arts curricula for more than two millennia.
  </Card>

  <Card title="Aristotle" icon="book-open">
    The philosopher whose _Rhetoric_ remains the single most studied treatment of the art. Aristotle systematised rhetorical proof (ethos, logos, pathos), defined rhetoric's relationship to dialectic, and gave the discipline its enduring taxonomic vocabulary.
  </Card>

  <Card title="Demetrius of Phalerum (c. 350–283 BCE)" icon="feather">
    Peripatetic scholar and statesman, traditionally credited with authorship of _On Style_ (_Peri Hermeneias_), which systematised the four levels of style — plain, grand, elegant, and forceful — influential throughout antiquity and beyond.
  </Card>
</CardGroup>

## Roman Rhetoricians

<CardGroup cols={2}>
  <Card title="Cicero" icon="columns">
    Rome's greatest orator and most prolific rhetorical theorist. His _De Inventione_, _De Oratore_, _Brutus_, and _Orator_ synthesised and extended the Greek tradition, while his speeches established the stylistic model for Latin prose that Renaissance humanists sought to recover.
  </Card>

  <Card title="Quintilian" icon="book">
    Author of the _Institutio Oratoria_ (c. 95 CE), the most comprehensive Latin handbook on rhetorical education ever written. Quintilian's vision of the _vir bonus dicendi peritus_ — the good man speaking well — tied rhetorical excellence inseparably to moral character.
  </Card>
</CardGroup>

## Medieval and Early Modern Theorists

<CardGroup cols={2}>
  <Card title="Bede (672 or 673–735 CE)" icon="church">
    The Venerable Bede brought classical rhetorical analysis to the study of biblical texts, preserving and adapting the tradition of figural analysis within the context of Christian hermeneutics and monastic scholarship.
  </Card>

  <Card title="Pizan, Christine de (1364–c. 1430)" icon="quill">
    Late-medieval French writer who deployed sophisticated rhetorical strategies in defence of women's capacities and virtue. Her _Book of the City of Ladies_ is one of the earliest sustained feminist rhetorical arguments in European literature.
  </Card>

  <Card title="Fell, Margaret (1614–1702)" icon="scroll">
    Quaker leader and writer whose _Women's Speaking Justified_ (1666) mounted one of the first extended public arguments for women's right to preach and participate in public discourse, drawing directly on biblical rhetoric.
  </Card>

  <Card title="Sidney, Sir Philip (1554–1586)" icon="pen-nib">
    Elizabethan poet and theorist whose _An Apology for Poetry_ defends imaginative literature on rhetorical grounds, arguing that poetry moves readers to virtuous action more effectively than either philosophy or history.
  </Card>
</CardGroup>

## Enlightenment and Nineteenth-Century Theorists

<CardGroup cols={2}>
  <Card title="Campbell, George (and Adam Smith)" icon="scales-balanced">
    George Campbell's _Philosophy of Rhetoric_ (1776) grounded persuasion in faculty psychology and Humean empiricism. Alongside Adam Smith's _Lectures on Rhetoric and Belles Lettres_, Campbell's work defined the dominant paradigm of eighteenth-century British and North American rhetorical theory.
  </Card>

  <Card title="Whately, Richard" icon="gavel">
    Anglican bishop and logician whose _Elements of Rhetoric_ (1828) returned the discipline to Aristotelian argumentation after a century of belletristic drift. His treatment of presumption and burden of proof remains influential in informal logic and debate theory.
  </Card>

  <Card title="Bentham, Jeremy (1748–1832)" icon="balance-scale">
    Utilitarian philosopher whose _Book of Fallacies_ and theory of _universal grammar_ brought linguistic analysis to bear on the rhetoric of political argument and legislation.
  </Card>

  <Card title="Emerson, Ralph Waldo (1803–1882)" icon="sun">
    American essayist and philosopher whose lectures and essays treated rhetoric as the art of making ideas felt — and whose lifelong engagement with Hugh Blair's _Lectures_ shaped his eventual break with belletristic tradition.
  </Card>
</CardGroup>

## Twentieth-Century Theorists

<CardGroup cols={2}>
  <Card title="Burke, Kenneth" icon="masks-theater">
    The most comprehensive rhetorical theorist of the twentieth century. Burke's dramatism, his theory of identification as the ground of rhetoric, his concept of consubstantiality, and his logological analysis of language have influenced scholars across the humanities and social sciences.
  </Card>

  <Card title="Olbrechts-Tyteca, Lucie (1899–1988)" icon="handshake">
    Belgian scholar whose collaboration with Chaïm Perelman produced _The New Rhetoric: A Treatise on Argumentation_ (1958), the most influential twentieth-century work on rhetorical theory, recentring rhetoric on the analysis of informal argumentation and audience.
  </Card>

  <Card title="Austin, J.L. (1911–1960)" icon="comment-dots">
    Ordinary-language philosopher and founder of speech act theory. Austin's distinction between performative and constative utterances — developed in _How to Do Things with Words_ — provided rhetoric with a powerful framework for analysing the actions language performs.
  </Card>

  <Card title="Barthes, Roland (1915–1980)" icon="book-bookmark">
    French critical theorist and the most influential French critic of the twentieth century. Barthes's work traversed structuralism, semiology, the theory of the text, and finally a deeply personal rhetoric of writing and reading.
  </Card>

  <Card title="De Man, Paul (1919–1983)" icon="eye">
    Proponent of deconstruction and theorist of the constitutively figurative nature of language. De Man argued that all language — including philosophical and theoretical writing — is rhetorical, forcing readers to acknowledge the irreducible gap between grammar and rhetoric.
  </Card>

  <Card title="Derrida, Jacques" icon="infinity">
    Philosopher of deconstruction whose critique of _logocentrism_ — the Western assumption that speech is more authentic than writing — fundamentally challenged rhetorical theory's traditional hierarchy of oral and written discourse.
  </Card>
</CardGroup>

## Twentieth-Century Composition Scholars

Several key theorists in the encyclopedia are associated specifically with composition studies and the teaching of writing — a field that inherited and reshaped rhetoric's pedagogical mission during the twentieth century.

| Theorist | Dates | Primary Contribution |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Berthoff, Ann E.** | b. 1924 | Grounded composition pedagogy in rhetoric and the philosophy of mind; challenged behaviourist models of writing |
| **Britton, James** | 1908–1994 | Distinguished expressive, transactional, and poetic functions of language; influential in writing-across-the-curriculum movements |
| **Chomsky, Noam** | b. 1928 | Transformational grammar and the theory of deep linguistic structure; challenged behaviourist accounts of language acquisition |
| **Lauer, Janice M.** | b. 1931 | Developed heuristic theory in composition, connecting classical invention to modern problem-solving |
| **Weaver, Richard M.** | 1910–1953 | American rhetorical theorist who tied the ethics of language to a broader social conservatism; influential in the revival of classical rhetoric |
| **DuBois, W.E.B.** | 1868–1963 | Major African-American writer and rhetorician; his work on race, discourse, and double-consciousness opened new dimensions of rhetorical analysis |
| **Truth, Sojourner** | 1790–1883 | Abolitionist and suffragist whose extemporaneous oratory — especially "Ain't I a Woman?" — demonstrated the power of situated embodied argument |
| **Cixous, Hélène** | b. 1937 | French feminist theorist of _écriture féminine_; argued that gender is inscribed in the very structures of discourse and rhetoric |
| **Rousseau, Jean-Jacques** | 1712–1778 | Enlightenment philosopher whose critique of rhetorical artifice in favour of natural expression challenged the discipline's foundational premises |

## How Theorist Entries Are Structured

Each theorist article in the encyclopedia follows a consistent pattern: it opens with a brief biographical identification, then describes the theorist's principal works and ideas, assesses their influence on subsequent rhetoric and related fields, and closes with a bibliography of primary and secondary sources. The **See Also** cross-references at the end of each entry link to related conceptual entries — so an entry on Aristotle will point toward **Enthymeme**, **Ethos**, **Logos**, **Pathos**, and **Stasis Theory**, while an entry on Burke will connect to **Dramatism**, **Identification**, **Consubstantiality**, and **Logology**.
