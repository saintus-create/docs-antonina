Rhetoric has a longer continuous history than almost any other intellectual discipline. From the law courts of fifth-century Syracuse to the networked discourse of the information age, scholars have returned again and again to the same core questions: How does language persuade? What makes communication effective and ethical? What is the relationship between words, knowledge, and power? The encyclopedia's 274 entries collectively trace that 2,500-year conversation. The overview below organises relevant articles by historical period so you can follow rhetoric's development in sequence or dip into any era that interests you.

---

## Classical Period (c. 467 BCE – 400 CE)

The discipline of rhetoric was formalised in classical antiquity — first in the Greek city-states of the fifth century BCE, then elaborated in the philosophical schools of fourth-century Athens, and finally carried to its fullest institutional expression in Rome. The classical inheritance — the five canons (invention, arrangement, style, memory, delivery), the three modes of proof (ethos, logos, pathos), and the taxonomy of rhetorical figures — shaped every subsequent era of rhetorical theory and practice.

**Key figures of the period:** Corax, who is credited with founding the discipline in Syracuse around 467 BCE; Isocrates, whose Athenian school rivalled Plato's Academy and offered a model of rhetorical education influential for more than two millennia; Aristotle, whose *Rhetoric* remains the single most studied treatment of the art; Cicero, who synthesised and extended the tradition in Rome; and Quintilian, whose *Institutio Oratoria* served as the definitive Roman handbook.

## Medieval Period (c. 400 – 1400 CE)

After the fall of the Western Roman Empire, rhetoric was transmitted and transformed within the institutions of the Christian church. The sermon became the dominant rhetorical genre; the *ars praedicandi* (art of preaching) replaced the *ars oratoria* as the most widely taught rhetorical form. Scholars such as Bede integrated classical precepts with scriptural exegesis, and the universities of the high Middle Ages embedded rhetoric within the *trivium* alongside grammar and logic. Scholasticism reconfigured the relationship between rhetoric and dialectic in ways that would shape debate long into the Renaissance.

## Renaissance and Early Modern Period (c. 1400 – 1700)

The Renaissance recovered and expanded the classical rhetorical tradition through the recovery of lost texts (including Cicero's *De Oratore* and Quintilian's *Institutio Oratoria* in complete form), the development of print culture, and intense attention to style and the figures of speech. Humanist educators such as Erasmus made *copia* — the productive abundance of expression — a central pedagogical goal. Peter Ramus controversially reassigned invention and arrangement to dialectic, leaving rhetoric as the art of style and delivery alone. Tudor England produced important rhetorical handbooks, and women writers such as Christine de Pizan and Margaret Fell began asserting distinctive rhetorical voices.

## Enlightenment and Eighteenth-Century Rhetoric (c. 1700 – 1830)

The eighteenth century produced one of the most sustained and influential reconfigurations of rhetorical theory in the discipline's history. Scottish rhetoricians — above all Hugh Blair, George Campbell, and Adam Smith — reoriented rhetoric around faculty psychology, the sublime, taste, and the emerging genre of belles lettres. Campbell's *Philosophy of Rhetoric* (1776) attempted to ground persuasion in the empirical science of human nature. Whately's *Elements of Rhetoric* (1828) returned to Aristotelian argumentation. The period also saw rhetoric migrate from the training of orators into the teaching of written composition — a shift with profound consequences for education on both sides of the Atlantic.

## Modern and Contemporary Rhetoric (1830 – Present)

The modern era has seen rhetoric simultaneously marginalised by composition studies' focus on written correctness and revitalised by scholars who returned to the discipline's broadest questions. The twentieth century in particular produced an explosion of rhetorical theory: Kenneth Burke's dramatism and identification, I.A. Richards' attempt to ground rhetoric in psychology and semantics, Chaïm Perelman and Lucie Olbrechts-Tyteca's *New Rhetoric*, and the engagements of poststructuralism, feminism, and cognitive science with the nature of persuasion and discourse. New sites of rhetorical practice — advertising, film, electronic media, political propaganda — joined the classical genres of deliberative, judicial, and epideictic speech.
